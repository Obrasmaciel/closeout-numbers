Markup vs Margin — the mistake that costs contractors their year

Free. No email required. The most expensive arithmetic error in small construction, and the table that fixes it.

The mistake

You add 20% to your costs and think you are making 20%.

You are making 16.7%.

That gap looks small on one job and it is not small over a year. On $800,000 of annual cost, a 20% markup returns $160,000 of profit. Pricing to an actual 20% margin on that same cost returns $200,000. Same work, same year, $40,000 difference — and you will not find it until the accountant does.

The two words

Markup is a percentage of your cost.

Margin is a percentage of your price.

Same job, two different denominators. Everyone in the trade uses both words, most people use them interchangeably, and the supplier quoting you, the client questioning you and the accountant reconciling you are frequently not talking about the same number.

Cost $10,000, markup 20% → price $12,000 → profit $2,000 → margin 16.7%.

To actually make a 20% margin on $10,000 of cost, you must price at $12,500. That is a 25% markup.

To get a target margin, divide your cost by (1 − margin).

Read the table once and the pattern is obvious: the higher your target margin, the further markup and margin drift apart. At 10% the error is trivial. At 35% it is enormous — and 35% is where a lot of small remodelers think they are operating.

Where it actually bites

Comparing yourself to someone else. A contractor telling you he runs "30%" may mean 30% margin (a 42.9% markup) or 30% markup (a 23.1% margin). Those are different businesses. Ask which one he means before you reprice your work against his number.

Discounting. Take 10% off a price built on a 20% markup and your margin drops from 16.7% to about 7.4% — you gave away more than half your profit for a number that sounded like a tenth of it.

Change orders. Applying your standard markup to a change order that carries more supervision per dollar than the base contract systematically underprices it. Change orders should usually carry a higher markup than the base job, not the same one.

Cost-plus contracts. "Cost plus 15%" is a markup, always. If your business plan needs a 15% margin, cost-plus-15 does not deliver it.

The part markup does not cover

Markup is not profit. It has to cover overhead first — rent, insurance, your phone, the truck, software, the office hours you don't bill, the estimates you lose.

If your overhead runs 15% of revenue and you price at a 20% markup (16.7% margin), your actual profit is under 2%. That is not a business, it is a job with extra risk. The overhead number has to come out of your own books before any markup table means anything.

The one number to find this week

Take last year's revenue. Take last year's direct job costs. Take everything else you spent — that is overhead.

Overhead ÷ revenue = your overhead rate.

Now you know the floor your margin has to clear before you have earned anything. Most contractors who do this exercise for the first time discover their floor is higher than the margin they have been pricing to.
