The Change Order Checklist

How to price, write and get paid for a change order — and the six that should never have been change orders.

Free. No email required.

The uncomfortable premise

Most contractors lose money on change orders twice. First by pricing them like base-contract work, and second by doing them before the paperwork exists.

A change order is not a small job. It is an interruption of a job that is already scheduled, staffed and priced. It costs more per dollar than the work around it, and pricing it at your standard markup systematically underprices it.

1. Before you price it

CheckWhy
Is this actually a change, or something you missed?If a perfect estimator with your day-one information would have included it, it is your miss and it comes out of your margin. Say so and move on — trying to bill your own omission is how you lose a client.
Is the scope written in nouns?"Upgrade the vanity" is not a scope. Model number, size, finish, who supplies it.
Who is asking, and can they authorize spending?The spouse who wants it and the spouse who pays are not always the same person.
Does it move the finish date?Almost every change does. If you don't say so now, you have silently accepted the old date.
Does it invalidate work already done or ordered?Restocking fees, demolition of new work, a cabinet order already in production.
Does it trigger permits or inspection?Structural, electrical service, plumbing relocation, egress. A change that needs a revised permit is a schedule event, not a price event.

3. What the written change order must contain

If any of these is missing, you are relying on goodwill:

1. A number — the change order number, sequential, referencing the original contract.

2. The scope, in nouns. What is being added, removed or substituted. Model numbers where they exist.

3. What is explicitly NOT included. The single most valuable line on the page.

4. The price, and whether it is fixed or time-and-materials with a not-to-exceed.

5. The schedule impact, in calendar days, stated even when it is zero.

6. Payment terms for this change — when it is billed, and whether it is due before the work starts.

7. A signature line and a date, both parties.

8. A statement that all other contract terms remain unchanged.

4. The rule that costs the most to break

Do not start the work before it is signed.

Every contractor knows this and most break it, because saying no feels like friction with a client who is standing right there. The unsigned change order is the single most common way small contractors do free work — and once the work exists, your leverage is gone. The client is no longer buying something, he is being asked to pay for something he already has.

If schedule pressure is genuinely real, a text message that says exactly what the work is and exactly what it costs, with a "reply YES to approve," is worth vastly more than a handshake. It is dated, it is written, and it is in the client's own words.

5. The six that should never have been change orders

These come up so consistently that they belong in the base contract as allowances or exclusions, not as surprises later:

1. Rot or damage found behind a wall or under a floor. Carry a disclosed contingency with a defined trigger, agreed before demo.

2. Bringing existing wiring or plumbing to current code once opened. State in the contract that code-required upgrades to existing systems are excluded from the base price.

3. Client selections that arrive late. Put a selection deadline in the contract with a stated consequence.

4. Substrate prep discovered after demo. Floor leveling, deflection, failed subfloor.

5. The feather-out — patching where new work meets existing floor, drywall and paint. Define where your finish stops.

6. Anything owned by a third party — HOA approval, utility scheduling, permit review time.

Naming these in the contract does not lose you the job. It is the clearest possible signal that you have done this before.

The number worth more than this list

Track what percentage of your revenue arrives as change orders, and what percentage of those you actually collected in full.

Most contractors find the second number is lower than they assumed, and that it is lower for a specific reason that repeats — unsigned work, or under-priced interruption. That is a fixable number, but only once it is written down.
