Labor Burden — what an hour of your crew actually costs

Free. No email required. The wage is not the cost, and 2,080 is not the divisor. Here is the arithmetic that gets you to the real number.

The mistake

You pay a carpenter $30 an hour. You price his time at $30 an hour plus markup.

You are already behind, and the markup will not save you, because the markup was applied to a cost that was never the cost.

Two separate things are missing. Most contractors who fix this fix only the first one.

One: things that sit on top of the wage. Payroll taxes, insurance, the benefits you offer. This is what people mean by "labor burden," and it is the easy half.

Two: the hours you pay for and cannot bill. Holidays, vacation, loading the truck, driving, the Tuesday it rained, the morning spent redoing someone else's work. You paid for all of it. You bill none of it. So your burdened annual cost has to be recovered over the hours you actually sell — not over every hour on the clock.

Miss the first and you are 20–40% light. Miss the second too and the number stops resembling reality.

What sits on top of the wage

Required by law. These are not optional and not negotiable:

ItemRate
Social Security (employer share)6.2% of wages, up to the annual wage base
Medicare (employer share)1.45% of wages, no wage cap
Federal unemployment (FUTA)6.0% on the first $7,000 per employee, less a state credit of up to 5.4% — 0.6% effective at the full credit
State unemployment (SUTA)Your state, your rate, your experience rating. Look it up.

The Social Security and Medicare employer shares together are 7.65% of wages. That is the floor of your burden and it applies to the first dollar.

Insurance. This is usually the biggest number after the wage itself, and it is the one that varies most:

  • Workers' compensation — priced per $100 of payroll, by trade classification and by your experience mod. A roofer's rate and a finish carpenter's rate are not in the same universe. Get the actual rate from your policy; do not use an average you read somewhere.
  • General liability — often rated on payroll or receipts, so it scales with the crew.
  • Any umbrella, tools, or vehicle coverage that scales with headcount.

What you chose to give. Health contribution, retirement match, paid time off, holidays, bonuses, per diem, phone allowance, take-home truck, tool allowance, boot or clothing allowance.

The cost of employing a person at all. Training and certifications, safety gear and its replacement, drug testing, the payroll service, the time you spend on hiring and on paperwork.

Every one of these is a real dollar leaving the account because that person works for you. If it would stop the day they left, it belongs in the burden.

The first multiplication

Add the annual cost of everything above, divide by annual wages, and you have your burden rate.

Burden rate = (total annual cost ÷ annual wages) − 1.

If the total lands at $87,000 against $62,400 of wages, the burden rate is 39%, and the loaded hourly is $87,000 ÷ 2,080 = $41.83.

That is the number most people stop at. It is still wrong.

The second multiplication — the one nobody does

2,080 hours is what you pay. It is not what you sell.

Take the 2,080 and remove every paid hour that does not land on a customer's invoice:

Holidaysyour number

Say those come to 380 hours. You are selling 1,700 hours, not 2,080.

$87,000 ÷ 1,700 = $51.18 an hour.

Same employee. Same $30 wage. The true cost of a billable hour is $51.18, not $30, and not the $41.83 that the burden rate alone suggested. Anyone bidding this crew at $30 plus markup is losing money on every hour and making it up on nothing.

Where the error compounds

Estimating in crew-hours. A 200-hour job priced at the wage instead of the true cost is not off by a rounding error. At the numbers above it is off by $4,236 of cost, before markup, before overhead, before profit.

Comparing your rate to a competitor's. His $45 an hour may be a real burdened-and-billable number or a wage with a guess on top. You cannot tell from outside, and you should not reprice your own work against a number you cannot see the construction of.

Change orders. Extra work is where non-productive hours hide — remobilizing, waiting on a decision, protecting finished work. Change-order hours are usually less productive than base-contract hours, so pricing them at the base labor rate underprices them twice.

Cost-plus. "Cost plus 15%" only works if "cost" is the burdened, billable-hour cost. Bill cost-plus off the raw wage and your 15% is quietly funding your own payroll taxes.

Deciding whether to hire. Adding a $30/hr employee is not a $62,400 decision. In this example it is an $87,000 decision, and it does not pay for itself until the crew sells the hours the divisor assumed.

The honest limits of this page

Two of these numbers we cannot give you, and anyone who hands you a national average for them is selling something:

  • Your workers' comp rate. It is set by trade class, state and your experience mod. It is on your policy declarations page.
  • Your state unemployment rate. Your state, your experience rating, and it moves.

And the productive-hour count is not something to estimate from feeling. If nobody records where the hours went, the divisor is a guess, and a guess in the divisor moves the answer more than any other input on this page.

The one number to find this week

Pull one employee's total cost for last year out of payroll — gross wages plus every employer-side dollar. Then pull the hours that were actually billed to jobs for that same person.

Divide the first by the second.

Compare it to the rate you have been using in estimates.

For most contractors doing this the first time, the gap is not a few dollars. It is the reason a busy year ended flat.
