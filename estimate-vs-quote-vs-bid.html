Estimate vs Quote vs Bid vs Proposal

Four words contractors use interchangeably, and the one that decides whether you can change the price later.

Free. No email required.

Why this is not pedantry

A homeowner who thinks he received a quote and a contractor who thinks he sent an estimate are going to have an argument. It will happen in week four, it will be about money, and the person who chose the word will lose it — because in most jurisdictions the wording, not the intention, decides what was promised.

The distinction is worth ten minutes of your attention once.

The four words

Estimate — a considered guess

An estimate is your best professional judgement of what a job will cost, given what you know now. It is not a fixed price. A reasonable variance is expected.

Use it when: the scope genuinely cannot be pinned down yet, when there is unknown work behind a wall, or when the client is still choosing finishes.

The word alone does not protect you. An estimate with no stated variance, no exclusions and no re-pricing trigger reads to a client exactly like a price. Write the tolerance down: "This is an estimate. The final invoice may vary from it by up to X%. Any change beyond that is presented as a change order for approval before the work happens."

Quote — a fixed price

A quote is an offer to do defined work for a defined amount. Once the client accepts it, in most places you have a contract at that number. The risk of your own mistake is yours.

Use it when: the scope is fully defined, you have your sub numbers in hand, and you have inspected the site.

A quote should always carry an expiry date. Material pricing moves; your quote should not outlive your supplier's.

Bid — a quote submitted in competition

Functionally a quote, submitted against others under a stated process. What makes it different is procedure: bid due dates, a defined scope package, sometimes bid bonds and formal addenda.

The trap: bidding against an incomplete drawing set. If your competitors interpreted an ambiguity more cheaply than you did, you lose on price for a reason that has nothing to do with your efficiency. Ask questions in writing during the bid period, and note your assumptions on the bid form.

Proposal — the scope plus the number, plus everything else

A proposal is the document. It contains the price (as estimate or quote), and also: what is included, what is excluded, the schedule, payment terms, and the assumptions you priced against.

The proposal is where you actually win or lose jobs. Two contractors sending the same number, one with a one-line text and one with a page that names the exclusions and the schedule, are not selling the same thing — and the second one is easier to trust and harder to argue with later.

The four lines that protect an estimate

Whatever you call the document, these four lines do most of the work:

1. "This price includes…" — nouns, not adjectives.

2. "This price excludes…" — the single most valuable paragraph you will ever write. Permit fees, unforeseen structural repair, code upgrades to existing systems, finish selections above the stated allowance.

3. "This price assumes…" — access, working hours, that the substrate is sound, that existing systems are functional, that selections arrive by a date.

4. "This price is valid until…" — a real date.

If any of those four is missing, the gap gets filled later by whoever argues better.

Allowances, honestly

An allowance is a number you inserted because the client had not decided yet. It is not a price; it is a placeholder that will be reconciled.

Say so explicitly, and say how: "Tile allowance of $X per square foot, supply only. Selections above this are billed at cost difference plus markup. Selections below reduce the contract by the difference."

Most allowance fights happen because only the first half of that sentence was written down.

What to do this week

Take the last proposal you sent and check it for the four lines. Most contractors find they have the first, sometimes the fourth, and almost never the second and third — which is exactly where the money leaks.
