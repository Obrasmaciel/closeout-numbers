Allowances — the line that decides who eats the difference

Free. No email required. An allowance is not a price. It is a placeholder that both of you will read differently in four months, and the arithmetic below is what that difference costs.

The mistake

You write one line in the estimate:

Tile allowance: $1,760

You mean: we have not picked tile yet, so I am carrying a number.

The homeowner reads: tile is included.

Nobody is lying. The line simply does not say enough, and the day the selection comes back at twice the number, you are having the conversation from behind — because the only written record says "$1,760" and says nothing about what happens next.

What an allowance actually is

An allowance is a dollar figure carried in the contract for a scope that exists but whose selection does not yet. Three things have to be true for it to survive contact with a real job:

1. It is stated in the contract — not in a text message, not in an email thread.

2. It says exactly what it covers, and by exclusion, what it does not.

3. It says how the difference is settled when the real number arrives.

Miss the third and you have not written an allowance. You have written a promise with a number next to it.

The worked example

The floor is 220 square feet. You carry a tile allowance at $8 a square foot: $1,760.

The client picks a tile at $14 a square foot. Here is what the order actually looks like:

Now the part that decides whether you also lose money on your own money.

Your contract runs at a 20% markup. Priced properly, that overage bills at $2,403.40 × 1.20 = $2,884.08. If the contract is silent on markup over the allowance — and most of them are — you bill the overage at cost and hand back $480.68 of gross profit for the privilege of ordering somebody else's tile.

The trap inside the allowance itself

Go back to the original line and work it the other way.

$1,760 has to cover material, freight and tax, over the same 21 boxes. Back the freight and the tax out: ($1,760 ÷ 1.08) − $180 = $1,449.63 of material, across 262.5 square feet.

That is $5.52 a square foot.

You wrote "$8 a square foot" and the client went shopping for $8 tile. The allowance never bought $8 tile. It bought $5.52 tile, and the gap was built in before anyone picked anything.

An allowance stated as a lump sum, or stated per square foot of floor rather than per square foot ordered, is short by the waste factor, the freight and the tax every single time. The number is wrong on the day you sign it.

The four questions every allowance has to answer in writing

1. Material only, or installed?

"Plumbing fixture allowance $3,000" — does that include setting them? If it does, and the client picks a wall-hung toilet with a carrier, the labor is not the labor you priced. If it does not, say material only, installation per the base contract scope so the labor conversation is a separate one.

2. What quantity, at what waste factor?

State the takeoff quantity and the waste percentage. "$8/sf across 253 sf ordered" cannot be misread. "$8/sf" can.

3. Freight, tax, delivery, returns?

Say whether the allowance is a delivered-to-site cost or a shelf price. Say who eats restocking on a return. Special-order tile is usually non-returnable, and the client who changes their mind should learn that from a contract clause rather than from you, in a driveway.

4. How is the difference settled — both directions?

Overage: billed at cost plus the same markup as the rest of the contract. Underage: credited — and say whether the credit is at cost or net of markup. Pick one, write it, be consistent. A client who catches you crediting less than you charge, without having said so up front, will not believe the next number either.

The fifth thing, which is not about money

A selection deadline, and what happens when it passes.

Allowances do not usually blow up on price. They blow up on time. The tile is picked three weeks late, the lead time is six weeks, and the tile setter you booked is now on another job. That delay costs more than the overage did, and it is invisible in every allowance clause that only talks about dollars.

Write the date the selection is due. Write what happens if it is not made: the contractor selects from a stated grade to keep the schedule, or the schedule moves and the resulting cost is a change order.

Allowance, contingency, change order — three different things

  • Allowance — known scope, unknown selection. Tile is going in. Which tile is open.
  • Contingency — unknown scope. What you find when the wall opens. Usually a percentage, and usually the client's money, not yours.
  • Change order — scope that was not in the contract at all, added later, priced at the time. See the change order checklist.

Contractors lose money by using an allowance where a contingency belonged: carrying a number for a condition they cannot see yet. If you do not know whether the scope exists, an allowance is the wrong instrument — it tells the client the work is priced.

Where allowances blow up most

  • Tile and flooring — waste factor, pattern, transitions, underlayment, the leveling you did not know you needed.
  • Plumbing fixtures — the fixture is the small number; the carrier, the valve body, the rough-in change are the big ones.
  • Lighting — quantity multiplies. Six cans became fourteen and the allowance was written per fixture.
  • Countertops — slab price is not the number. Template, fabrication, edge profile, sink cutout, seams and the minimum-square-foot charge are.
  • Cabinet hardware — small unit price, large count, and the client shops it last.
  • Appliances — a selection change that moves a gas line or a dedicated circuit is not an appliance cost, and it is not in the appliance allowance.

Every one of these has the same shape: the allowance covers the object, and the selection changes the work around the object.

The one habit that closes this

Record what each allowance line actually cost at closeout, next to what you carried.

After four jobs you stop guessing at the tile number. You know that your clients pick tile at $11 to $15, not $8, and you carry $13 — which is not a bigger allowance, it is an honest one. The bid gets more competitive, not less, because you stop padding the lines you are unsure about and start pricing the number you have seen.

That is the whole difference between an estimate you repeat and an estimate that learns.
