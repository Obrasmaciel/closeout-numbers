Deck Estimate Checklist

Every line a residential deck usually needs — and the ones that get left out.

Free. No email required. Built for the contractor pricing a ground-level to second-storey wood or composite deck.

How to use this

Price the deck the way you normally do, then read this against it. You are looking for the four or five lines that make you stop.

Items marked often missed don't appear in a square-foot price and become your problem after the concrete is poured.

1. Before anything is dug

Line itemNote
Permit fee and plan submissionDecks over a certain height or area almost always need one
Engineering or stamped plan, where requiredAttached decks in many jurisdictions; a schedule item, not just a cost
HOA or historic approvalOften missed. Not a cost line — a schedule line, and it can kill a design
Setback and easement checkCheap to verify, catastrophic to discover after footings
Utility locate, public and privateOften missed. The private-side locate is billable and the damage is yours
Site survey if the property line is close
Existing-conditions photos before you startFree, and it settles the "that crack was already there" conversation

Line itemNote
Access for equipment, or all-hand diggingOften missed. A gate too narrow for an auger changes the labor line entirely
Tree roots, buried debris, old footingsCarry a disclosed contingency for what is under the grass
Lawn and landscape protection, and restorationOften missed. The client will notice the ruts before he notices the deck
Spoil removal from footing holesIt has to go somewhere, and "spread it out back" is a conversation, not a plan
Slope and drainage away from the house
Temporary power for tools

3. Foundation and structure

Line itemNote
Footing depth to local frost lineNot the depth you used on the last job in a different county
Concrete volume including over-digOften missed. Holes are never the theoretical size
Post bases, standoff hardware, anchorsCode requires standoffs on most jobs; they are not free
Ledger attachment: lags or through-bolts, and flashingOften missed as a system — the flashing is what stops the rot lawsuit
Removing siding to install the ledger, and reinstating itOften missed
Rim joist condition behind the sidingThe most common hidden discovery on an attached deck
Beams, joists, blocking, and joist hangersHangers and structural screws add up faster than most estimates carry
Joist tape on joist topsCheap, and it materially extends framing life
Lateral load connectorsCode in many jurisdictions, invisible in most bids
Freestanding-deck bracing, if not ledger-attached

4. Decking, rail and stairs

Line itemNote
Decking waste factor for pattern, picture frame, or diagonal10% does not cover a diagonal layout
Fasteners: hidden clips vs. face screwsOften missed. Hidden fastener systems cost several times face screws in both material and labor
Fascia, riser boards and trimPriced per linear foot, forgotten in a square-foot number
Railing: posts, infill, top cap, and the hardwareComposite and cable railing are a different price bracket entirely
Rail post attachment blocking and hardwareOften missed. Code-compliant post attachment is a detail with real labor in it
Stairs: stringers, treads, risersEvery stair is more labor per square foot than the deck
Stair landing pad or footingOften missed. Stairs have to land on something rated
Stair railing and graspable handrailA separate code requirement from guard rail
Lighting: post caps, stair lights, transformer and wiringOften missed. Low voltage still needs a circuit and a run

The number worth more than this list

Price the next three decks by phase — site work, foundation, framing, decking, rail, stairs, closeout. At closeout, write down what each phase actually cost.

Decks are unusually good for this exercise because they repeat. Three decks will tell you which phase you consistently underprice, and for most contractors it is the same one every time: stairs and rail, where the labor per square foot is nothing like the field.
